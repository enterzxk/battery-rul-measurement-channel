## Abstract
Cross-condition battery remaining-useful-life models are usually evaluated leave-one-condition-out, which gives the model no labels from the target condition. In deployment, labels arrive first. We study their allocation at a fixed budget of eight rows, from one donor cell or spread across two.

Across 64 LFP cells in 11 conditions and 248 cells in 63 conditions, with remaining life in cycles, moving from one to two donor cells reduced mean absolute calibration error by 16.67 cycles (11/11, Holm p = 0.0002) and 0.156 units (44/57, Holm 0.0003) under affine calibration, and by 6.63 (8/11, n.s.) and 0.085 (39/57, Holm 0.0005) under isotonic calibration. The effect is estimator-dependent: a nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest point moved the opposite way on both datasets (-7.82 and -0.063); borrowing the residual attenuated that harm to +0.53 and +0.001.

The effect is not a unit artifact, is not confined to linear calibration, and keeps its sign across all four remaining-life quartiles. It has population boundaries: on a query subset defined by donor availability, affine and isotonic effects reverse under both donor pools, and no pool-by-count interaction survives correction. Leave-one-condition-out selection recovered two donors in every fold, without beating the best fixed strategy. No universal donor-count or calibrator recommendation follows. Within the calibration layer the same contrast is sensitive to fewer choices than it first appears. Restricting evaluation to the late-life quartile reverses its sign on both datasets (-208% and -218%), while two null manipulations that change nothing move it by 10 to 19% and the two remaining choices stay inside that range; and one of the five decisions turns out never to have been implemented, its reported 274% being an artefact of a single Monte-Carlo draw.
## 1 Introduction

Battery life models are validated by holding out an operating condition and reporting error on it. The protocol is clean and it answers a real question: how well does this model transfer to a condition it has never seen? But it is not the question a deployment engineer faces. A newly deployed condition produces data immediately, and the practical question is how much of it must be labelled before the model becomes usable.

That question has two axes. The number of labelled rows k can be increased at almost no cost, because a completed cell trajectory can be mined retrospectively for many label points. The number of distinct donor cells m is expensive: each additional donor is another cell that must be run to end of life before its labels exist. If the two axes were interchangeable, one would always buy the cheap one. Our starting hypothesis was that they are not, and that there is a floor on the row axis.

The floor did not appear. Rows kept helping beyond the point where our design had power to detect saturation. What did appear, and what this paper is about, is that the donor axis has an effect whose sign depends on the estimator and whose magnitude depends on the population you evaluate it on.

The paper makes three claims, and only three.

First, at a fixed budget of eight labelled rows from other cells of the target condition, increasing the donor count from one to two produced modest average improvements for affine and isotonic calibration on two datasets, with unequal statistical support.

Second, the donor-count effect depends on the estimator. A nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest calibration point showed the opposite direction; borrowing the residual instead attenuated that harm. These controls constrain the possible mechanisms without establishing that aggregation alone causes the difference.

Third, the full-population benefit has population boundaries. Stratifying by RUL quartile preserves the sign of every effect, but a subset of queries selected by a donor-availability constraint reverses the affine and isotonic effects under both donor pools.

## 2 Related work

Supervision budgets for cross-condition battery models have been probed before, but usually as a robustness check rather than as the object of study. Zhao (2026) compares budgets of five, ten and twenty target cells for scalar cycle-life transfer and concludes that large budgets are unnecessary; its unit is a cell, and its target is a single number per cell. Su et al. (2025) frame the budget as a percentage of lifetime data and report that a small fraction suffices for state-of-health transfer. BatLiNet (2025) ensembles the median prediction over a reference group of 32 source-side cells, reports that both the mean and the variance of the error fall as that group grows, and notes that inference remains fast even at 64; its references are drawn from training cells and carry no target labels.

Our setting differs in three ways that matter for interpretation. The budget is counted in labelled rows from *other cells of the target condition*, so calibration and evaluation cells are disjoint by construction. The target is a trajectory rather than a scalar, so one donor contributes many label points. And the comparison is made at an exactly matched row budget, so a donor-count difference cannot be attributed to row count.

The estimator dependence we observe is a special case of a known phenomenon: local methods are sensitive to the density and homogeneity of the calibration set, while parametric fits average over it. Work on local gradient correction for tabular regression makes the same distinction. We do not claim a new estimator; we use the contrast as a probe.

That unspecified protocol choices can change a reported result is not a new observation, in this field or outside it. Peng, Wang and Zhang (2026) show that one column's normalisation convention moves RMSE by up to 108% and reverses the sign of a benefit, from -5.3% to +4.5%, depending solely on whether features are normalised per cell or against source-fitted statistics, and that replacing an offline author protocol with a strict online reader reverses the sign again, from -16.8% and -19.1% to +4.3% and +10.4%. Their normalisation manipulation is itself run as a 2x2 over feature richness and normalisation; the two manipulations are not crossed with each other and no interaction term is reported. In the wider machine-learning literature the same phenomenon is documented for preprocessing fitted before splitting, for record-wise against subject-wise evaluation, and for aggregation choices, and multiverse analyses in software engineering report that fewer than one in five hundred pipeline variants reproduces a published result.

Peng et al. therefore already establish that choices of this kind reverse signs, and already cross two of them. Our objective is narrower: to measure how sensitive one fixed-budget donor-allocation contrast is to the calibration and evaluation choices around it, and to ask which of the resulting patterns recur across two datasets rather than in one.

## 3 Protocol

### 3.1 Data

UConn-ISU-ILCC: 64 LFP cells in 11 operating-condition groups, characterised at up to 77 reference performance tests, each providing a 100 s pulse at 20/50/90% state of charge in both directions and a constant-current discharge curve. RUL is expressed in cumulative cycles: the per-interval cycle count is a segment length, and its cumulative sum gives a monotone aging clock (Spearman correlation between state of health and cumulative cycles is -1.0000 in 52 of 64 cells). Usable queries: 2,813, with RUL from 0 to 8,425 cycles (median 2,234).

ISU-ILCC (second dataset): 248 of 251 cells parse, in 63 condition groups, 5,497 reference-test rows of which 3,422 are usable for RUL; every cell reaches 80% state of health. Features are four capacity channels. RUL is in reference-test counts (0-42) because a cycle-count axis is not distributed with this dataset.

### 3.2 Predictor and calibration

One gradient-boosted regressor per dataset, held fixed throughout; all calibration experiments are post-processing of its leave-one-condition-out predictions. A second frozen predictor (ridge regression, features standardised inside each training fold) is used as a control.

Calibration maps the predictor output to the target using k labelled rows drawn only from cells other than the one being evaluated. Four calibrators: an intercept-only shift, an affine map, isotonic regression, and a nearest-neighbour rule that borrows either the raw label or the residual of the nearest calibration point in prediction space.

### 3.3 Statistics

The statistical unit is the operating condition, never the cell or the query. Unless stated otherwise, queries are averaged within each cell and then across cells, so every arm uses identical weights; where an earlier run used query-weighted averaging instead, the text says so and reports both. Monte-Carlo draws are aggregated with the mean (primary) and the median (mandatory sensitivity). Multiplicity is controlled by Holm correction with the cumulative maximum, over pre-declared families.

### 3.4 Protocol lock

Three degrees of freedom were frozen before the main experiments: the aggregation rule over Monte-Carlo draws, donor eligibility, and the sampling scheme. A fourth, the scope of the affine fit, was intended to be frozen at the same time; the lock compared the two branches at 0.018 MAE against a within-configuration seed standard deviation of 0.0063, but on re-reading the lock's code neither branch shares a single fit across the cells of a condition, so that comparison measures a change in random path rather than a change of scope, and we do not treat the scope as having been locked or as having been varied (Section 5). One choice was not pre-registered: mean aggregation over draws was adopted as the primary summary after inspecting results, with the median retained as a mandatory sensitivity, and the main table reports the mean with median values given in Section 4.1. We state this as a post-hoc decision rather than a pre-registered one.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Donor count, two datasets

At an exactly matched budget of eight labelled rows, with a common donor pool and the same evaluable cells in both arms. Mean and median aggregation agree in sign and in which calibrators reach correction.

The same effects computed in reference-test counts on UConn (affine +0.21, isotonic +0.07, 1-NN -0.06) are within 1.44% versus 1.43% of baseline for isotonic, so the conclusion does not depend on the unit.

The single-donor row axis did not reach the pre-registered equivalence margin. Fixing one donor and adding rows from k = 16 to k = 64 changed error by +0.108 and +0.146 (mean, two seeds) and +0.033 and +0.074 (median). The mean point estimates exceed 0.05 and every interval's upper bound does; the two median point estimates do not, so the margin is crossed by the intervals rather than by every point estimate. A separate single-donor probe in an earlier configuration gave -0.062 (p = 0.0033, 17 paired units) and was read at the time as evidence of saturation at k of about 16. Its subtraction order is the reverse of the one used here, so it agrees in direction with the present estimates rather than opposing them; what we do not sustain is the saturation reading, which rests on the same thin donor-availability base. The test is weak for a structural reason: only 9 of 64 cells have at least 64 rows and they fall in three conditions, so the primary test has three paired units. We therefore report it as inconclusive rather than as evidence that rows do not matter.

### 4.2 Estimator dependence

At the same budget and on the same queries, a nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest calibration point moves in the opposite direction on both datasets. Replacing raw-label borrowing with residual borrowing, in which the nearest point contributes y - p and that residual is added to the test prediction, attenuates the harm to +0.53 (UConn) and +0.001 (ISU).

This is consistent with a level-versus-shape distinction: estimators that aggregate over calibration points average out donor-specific offsets, while a nearest-neighbour rule selects one point and therefore inherits the offset without averaging. The comparison is a probe, not a method: residual borrowing is the worst of the four calibrators on ISU (2.939) but not on UConn, where the raw-label rule is worse (535.51 against 528.26).

We note an algebraic caveat. Residual borrowing removes the neighbour's prediction displacement but retains its residual bias, and at the neighbour's own prediction the two rules coincide. The contrast therefore constrains possible mechanisms; it does not identify aggregation as the cause.

### 4.3 Population boundaries

Stratifying queries by RUL quartile (cut points 1,144, 2,234, 3,430 cycles) preserves the sign of every effect: intercept +28.81/+21.08/+17.12/+17.55, affine +11.53/+27.00/+27.88/+22.39, isotonic +7.29/+12.83/+9.39/+7.39, 1-NN -10.41/-7.41/-5.88/-11.48. Sixteen cells, no sign exception. Individual cells are underpowered at n = 11 conditions.

A donor-availability constraint, in which only donors whose trajectory is complete by the target's current cycle may be used, restricts evaluation to 199 queries. Under cell-macro weighting the affine and isotonic effects on that subset are -21.57 (2/11 conditions improved) and -24.92 (0/11); an earlier run of the same comparison that weighted queries rather than cells gave -21.76 and -16.85, and the isotonic figure differs materially between the two weightings, which we report rather than reconcile. A factorial comparison on those same 199 queries, crossing donor pool (restricted or unrestricted) with donor count and using cell-macro weighting throughout, finds no interaction: all four pool-by-count interactions are null after Holm correction, and the unrestricted pool also reverses on that subset (-28.03 affine, -23.03 isotonic). The reversal therefore belongs to the query subset, not to the constraint.

### 4.4 Controls

An uncalibrated arm is invariant to donor count (difference exactly zero, 0/11), as it must be, since it uses no labels. An intercept-only calibrator, which estimates no slope, also benefits from a second donor (+21.14 on the gradient-boosted predictor (631.03 to 609.89), +31.86 on ridge (597.21 to 565.35)), which rules out an explanation based on slope conditioning alone.

The ranking of calibrators is not stable across base predictors: affine passes correction on the gradient-boosted predictor while isotonic does not, and the reverse holds on ridge. No claim about which calibrator is best is made.

### 4.5 Practical utility

A leave-one-condition-out procedure that selects the calibrator and donor count on training conditions and evaluates on the held-out condition chose two donors in every fold on every dataset: 11 of 11 on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor, 11 of 11 with ridge (ten selecting affine with two donors and one selecting isotonic with two), and 57 of 57 on ISU. Its error matched the best fixed strategy exactly on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor (454.33) and on ISU (2.470), and was 7.40 worse on UConn with ridge (499.98 against 492.58, p = 0.34).

Comparing best achievable with one donor against best achievable with two: 462.04 against 454.33 on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor, 514.00 against 492.58 with ridge, and 2.542 against 2.470 on ISU. The UConn isotonic two-donor figure is 454.33 in this selection experiment and 453.33 in the arm table of Section 4.1; the two come from separate runs with different draw counts and are not interchangeable. The donor decision is therefore learnable from training conditions alone and worth about 2 to 4% of error, but it does not beat the best fixed strategy, because it converges to it.

## 5 What the calibration layer is sensitive to

The calibration layer is post-processing: it takes a fixed predictor's output and maps it to the target using a handful of labelled rows. Its reported effect therefore depends on choices that are not part of the method and are rarely stated. We scanned those choices on one headline contrast - UConn, remaining life in cycles, eight labels, affine calibration, one donor against two - varying one at a time, and repeated the scan on ISU. The scan has its own baseline, +18.34 on UConn and +0.132 on ISU, which differs from the main-table contrasts of +16.67 and +0.156 because it is a separate run with its own draw count and, on ISU, a common-support cell set of 223 cells in both arms; only the within-run changes below are interpretable.

| Decision varied | UConn | change | ISU | change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| baseline: cell-macro, mean over draws, per-cell affine, all queries | +18.34 | - | +0.132 | - |
| aggregation order changed to query pooling | +20.57 | +12% | +0.126 | -4% |
| draw aggregation changed to median | +17.26 | -6% | +0.082 | -38% |
| evaluation restricted to the late-life quartile | -19.86 | -208% | -0.155 | -218% |
| null: same configuration, different seed | +15.62 | -15% | +0.145 | +11% |
| null: floor(k/m) allocation, identical row counts | +16.55 | -10% | +0.156 | +19% |
| calibration rows relaxed to all donor rows | +68.65 | +274% | not run | - |

The two null rows are the point of the table. Both change nothing about the specification: the first reruns the baseline from a different random stream, and the second switches to an allocation rule that assigns the same number of rows at eight labels and one or two donors. Together they move the contrast by 10 and 15% on UConn and by 11 and 19% on ISU. That is the scale at which this scan can resolve anything.

One row is not what its label said. The calibration-row row does not vary the scope of the fit: the affine map is still fitted separately for each evaluated cell, and what changes is that the calibration set becomes every row of the sampled donor trajectories instead of eight. At a matched 200 draws that change is worth +32% on UConn, from +18.34 to +24.17, so the reported +274% is not a row-budget effect either; it is dominated by the single draw the original arm used, since at the same eight-row budget one draw gives +15.62 where 200 draws give +18.34, and with all donor rows one draw gives +54.77 where 200 give +24.17. A scope comparison that keeps eight labels while keeping the evaluated cell disjoint from the donors cannot be constructed in this design without either giving every cell in a condition the same eight rows - in which case the two scopes are the same fit - or calibrating a cell on its own labels. We therefore withdraw the scope factor rather than repair it, and we take the collapse of its reported magnitude as this section's main lesson.

What survives the null is the evaluation population. Restricting evaluation to the late-life quartile reverses the sign of the contrast on both datasets, from +18.34 to -19.86 on UConn (-208%) and from +0.132 to -0.155 on ISU (-218%), against null ranges of 10 to 15% and 11 to 19%. Holding the fit at per-cell and varying only the population gives +17.11 on all queries (p = 0.0006, 10 of 11 conditions improved) and -16.27 on the late-life quartile (p = 0.246, 4 of 11). The late-life contrast is not significant in either direction, so the scan records that the reported direction is population-dependent, not that the second donor is harmful.

Three qualifications apply. The late-life population here is the lowest quartile of remaining life computed within each condition, whereas the quartile stratification of Section 4.3 uses cut points computed over the pooled sample; these are different populations and we state both rather than reconcile them. The restriction changes the estimand: it is a retrospective evaluation on a subset defined by true remaining life, not an executable online selection rule. And the median row of the scan takes a median across draws for each query before averaging, whereas the median rows of Section 4.1 take the median of a condition's cell-macro error; median and averaging do not commute, so the two are distinct summaries of different quantities.

The two remaining decisions, aggregation order and draw aggregation, move the contrast by +12 and -6% on UConn and by -4 and -38% on ISU. Aggregation order reverses direction between datasets and stays inside the null on both. Draw aggregation keeps its sign but is four times larger on ISU, where the median arm is also the only one that loses significance (p = 0.0594). We do not rank the two, and we do not claim that either can be standardised without loss.

Figure 1 shows the one-at-a-time scan for both datasets. Its two bars labelled fit scope and row allocation carry exactly the labels this section withdraws; read them as the calibration-row-budget change and the null manipulation respectively.

The practical reading is therefore narrower than a ranking. A disagreement between two studies about whether an extra donor helps need not reflect a difference in data; it can reflect the population over which the effect is averaged. Of the five post-processing choices we set out to compare, one is resolvable well outside the null, one turned out not to be implemented at all, one is the null control itself, and two stay inside the range that control defines. We report the resolution the scan actually has rather than an ordering it cannot support.
## 6 Limitations

ISU supplies 63 conditions in total; 57 enter the donor-count contrasts because six have fewer than two eligible donor cells under the eight-row budget, and the exclusion is applied identically to every arm. Two datasets, two base predictors, one task definition. The condition is the statistical unit, giving n = 11 on UConn and 57 on ISU for the main contrasts, and a minimum detectable effect near 0.30 MAE on UConn. The single-donor row test has three paired units and cannot decide saturation either way. The donor effect is small in relative terms (1.4 to 6% of baseline), the calibrator ranking is unstable across base predictors, and the residual-borrowing probe is diagnostic rather than a method. The sensitivity scan of Section 5 is bounded by two null manipulations that move the contrast by 10 to 19%, so only its evaluation-population factor is resolvable, and its fit-scope factor could not be implemented without either leakage or degeneracy. ISU RUL is in reference-test counts, not cycles, because no cycle axis is distributed with that dataset. Nothing here speaks to how the conclusions change with a different predictor family or with physical, calendar-time end-of-life definitions.

## 7 Conclusion

At a fixed budget of eight labels drawn from other cells of the target operating condition, a second donor cell buys a small and estimator-dependent improvement in calibration error. The direction reverses for an estimator that borrows a single raw label, survives stratification by remaining life, and reverses again on a query subset defined by donor availability. The donor-count decision can be learned from training conditions and is worth a few percent, but it does not displace the best fixed strategy. We report these boundaries rather than a recommendation.
