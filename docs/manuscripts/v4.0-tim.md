# Donor Allocation at a Fixed Label Budget: Estimator Dependence and Population Boundaries in Battery RUL Calibration

> Draft v4.0 — 2026-11-08. Every number traceable to `.research/exp/EXP-001..021`. Scope fixed by CD-015: three claims only.

## Abstract
Cross-condition evaluation of battery remaining-useful-life models is usually reported under leave-one-condition-out, a protocol that gives the model no labels from the target condition. In deployment, labels always arrive first. We study their allocation at a fixed budget: eight labelled rows, from one donor cell or spread across two.

Across 64 LFP cells in 11 operating conditions and 248 cells in 63 conditions, with remaining life in cumulative cycles, moving from one to two donor cells reduced mean absolute calibration error by 16.67 cycles (11/11 conditions, Holm p = 0.0002) and 0.156 units (44/57, Holm 0.0003) under affine calibration, and by 6.63 (8/11, not significant) and 0.085 (39/57, Holm 0.0005) under isotonic calibration. The effect is estimator-dependent: a nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest point moved the opposite way on both datasets (-7.82 and -0.063); borrowing the residual instead attenuated that harm to +0.53 and +0.001.

The effect is not a unit artifact, is not confined to linear calibration, and keeps its sign across all four remaining-life quartiles (16 of 16 cells). It has population boundaries: on a query subset defined by donor availability, affine and isotonic effects reverse under both restricted and unrestricted donor pools, and no pool-by-count interaction survives correction. Leave-one-condition-out selection recovered two donors in 11/11, 10/11 and 57/57 folds, without beating the best fixed strategy. No universal donor-count or calibrator recommendation follows. We also report five implementation defects in our own analysis, each found by independent review of our code, not by self-inspection.
## 1 Introduction

Battery life models are validated by holding out an operating condition and reporting error on it. The protocol is clean and it answers a real question: how well does this model transfer to a condition it has never seen? But it is not the question a deployment engineer faces. A newly deployed condition produces data immediately, and the practical question is how much of it must be labelled before the model becomes usable.

That question has two axes. The number of labelled rows k can be increased at almost no cost, because a completed cell trajectory can be mined retrospectively for many label points. The number of distinct donor cells m is expensive: each additional donor is another cell that must be run to end of life before its labels exist. If the two axes were interchangeable, one would always buy the cheap one. Our starting hypothesis was that they are not, and that there is a floor on the row axis.

The floor did not appear. Rows kept helping beyond the point where our design had power to detect saturation. What did appear, and what this paper is about, is that the donor axis has an effect whose sign depends on the estimator and whose magnitude depends on the population you evaluate it on.

The paper makes three claims, and only three.

First, at a fixed budget of eight labelled rows from other cells of the target condition, increasing the donor count from one to two produced modest average improvements for affine and isotonic calibration on two datasets, with unequal statistical support.

Second, the donor-count effect depends on the estimator. A nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest calibration point showed the opposite direction; borrowing the residual instead attenuated that harm. These controls constrain the possible mechanisms without establishing that aggregation alone causes the difference.

Third, the full-population benefit has population boundaries. Stratifying by RUL quartile preserves the sign of every effect, but a subset of queries selected by a donor-availability constraint reverses the affine and isotonic effects under both donor pools.

## 2 Related work

Supervision budgets for cross-condition battery models have been probed before, but usually as a robustness check rather than as the object of study. Zhao (2026) scans five, ten and twenty target cells for scalar cycle-life transfer and concludes that large budgets are unnecessary; its unit is a cell, and its target is a single number per cell. Su et al. (2025) frame the budget as a percentage of lifetime data and report that a small fraction suffices for state-of-health transfer. BatLiNet (2025) varies the number of source-side reference cells from one to sixty-four to reduce variance in its median prediction, and settles on sixty-four for inference speed; its references carry no target labels.

Our setting differs in three ways that matter for interpretation. The budget is counted in labelled rows from *other cells of the target condition*, so calibration and evaluation cells are disjoint by construction. The target is a trajectory rather than a scalar, so one donor contributes many label points. And the comparison is made at an exactly matched row budget, so a donor-count difference cannot be attributed to row count.

The estimator dependence we observe is a special case of a known phenomenon: local methods are sensitive to the density and homogeneity of the calibration set, while parametric fits average over it. Work on local gradient correction for tabular regression makes the same distinction. We do not claim a new estimator; we use the contrast as a probe.

## 3 Protocol

### 3.1 Data

UConn-ISU-ILCC: 64 LFP cells in 11 operating-condition groups, characterised at up to 77 reference performance tests, each providing a 100 s pulse at 20/50/90% state of charge in both directions and a constant-current discharge curve. RUL is expressed in cumulative cycles: the per-interval cycle count is a segment length, and its cumulative sum gives a monotone aging clock (Spearman correlation between state of health and cumulative cycles is -1.0000 in 52 of 64 cells). Usable queries: 2,813, with RUL from 0 to 8,425 cycles (median 2,234).

ISU-ILCC (second dataset): 248 of 251 cells parse, in 63 condition groups, 5,497 reference-test rows of which 3,422 are usable for RUL; every cell reaches 80% state of health. Features are four capacity channels. RUL is in reference-test counts (0-42) because a cycle-count axis is not distributed with this dataset.

### 3.2 Predictor and calibration

One gradient-boosted regressor per dataset, held fixed throughout; all calibration experiments are post-processing of its leave-one-condition-out predictions. A second frozen predictor (ridge regression, features standardised inside each training fold) is used as a control.

Calibration maps the predictor output to the target using k labelled rows drawn only from cells other than the one being evaluated. Four calibrators: an intercept-only shift, an affine map, isotonic regression, and a nearest-neighbour rule that borrows either the raw label or the residual of the nearest calibration point in prediction space.

### 3.3 Statistics

The statistical unit is the operating condition, never the cell or the query. Within a condition, queries are averaged within each cell and then across cells, so every arm uses identical weights. Monte-Carlo draws are aggregated with the mean (primary) and the median (mandatory sensitivity). Multiplicity is controlled by Holm correction with the cumulative maximum, over pre-declared families.

### 3.4 Protocol lock

Four degrees of freedom were frozen before the main experiments: the aggregation rule over Monte-Carlo draws, the scope of the affine fit (per evaluated cell or shared per condition), donor eligibility, and the sampling scheme. Maximum cross-configuration drift was 0.018 MAE against a within-configuration seed standard deviation of 0.0063.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Donor count, two datasets

At an exactly matched budget of eight labelled rows, with a common donor pool and the same evaluable cells in both arms:

| Dataset | Calibrator | m = 1 | m = 2 | Difference | Conditions improved | Holm |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| UConn (cycles) | affine | 516.88 | 500.21 | +16.67 | 11/11 | 0.0002 |
| UConn (cycles) | isotonic | 459.95 | 453.33 | +6.63 | 8/11 | 0.34 |
| UConn (cycles) | 1-NN | 535.51 | 543.33 | -7.82 | 2/11 | 0.036 |
| ISU (RPT) | affine | 2.827 | 2.670 | +0.156 | 44/57 | 0.0003 |
| ISU (RPT) | isotonic | 2.551 | 2.466 | +0.085 | 39/57 | 0.0005 |
| ISU (RPT) | 1-NN | 2.756 | 2.819 | -0.063 | 7/57 | 0.0000 |

The same effects computed in reference-test counts on UConn (affine +0.21, isotonic +0.07, 1-NN -0.06) are within 1.44% versus 1.43% of baseline for isotonic, so the conclusion does not depend on the unit.

The single-donor row axis did not saturate under the pre-registered equivalence margin. Fixing one donor and adding rows from k = 16 to k = 64 changed error by +0.108 and +0.146 (mean, two seeds) and +0.033 and +0.074 (median), all above that margin. The test is weak for a structural reason: only 9 of 64 cells have at least 64 rows and they fall in three conditions, so it has three paired units and is reported as inconclusive rather than as evidence that rows do not matter.

### 4.2 Estimator dependence

At the same budget and on the same queries, a nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest calibration point moves in the opposite direction on both datasets. Replacing raw-label borrowing with residual borrowing, in which the nearest point contributes y - p and that residual is added to the test prediction, attenuates the harm to +0.53 (UConn) and +0.001 (ISU).

This is consistent with a level-versus-shape distinction: estimators that aggregate over calibration points average out donor-specific offsets, while a nearest-neighbour rule selects one point and therefore inherits the offset without averaging. The comparison is a probe, not a method: residual borrowing is the worst of the four calibrators in absolute terms (528.26 on UConn, 2.939 on ISU).

We note an algebraic caveat. Residual borrowing removes the neighbour's prediction displacement but retains its residual bias, and at the neighbour's own prediction the two rules coincide. The contrast therefore constrains possible mechanisms; it does not identify aggregation as the cause.

### 4.3 Population boundaries

Stratifying queries by RUL quartile (cut points 1,144, 2,234, 3,430 cycles) preserves the sign of every effect: intercept +28.81/+21.08/+17.12/+17.55, affine +11.53/+27.00/+27.88/+22.39, isotonic +7.29/+12.83/+9.39/+7.39, 1-NN -10.41/-7.41/-5.88/-11.48. Sixteen cells, no sign exception. Individual cells are underpowered at n = 11 conditions.

A donor-availability constraint, in which only donors whose trajectory is complete by the target's current cycle may be used, restricts evaluation to 199 queries and reverses the affine and isotonic effects (-21.76 and -16.85, 2/11 and 0/11 conditions improved). A factorial comparison on those same 199 queries, crossing donor pool (restricted or unrestricted) with donor count, finds no interaction: all four pool-by-count interactions are null after Holm correction, and the unrestricted pool also reverses on that subset. The reversal therefore belongs to the query subset, not to the constraint.

### 4.4 Controls

An uncalibrated arm is invariant to donor count (difference exactly zero, 0/11), as it must be, since it uses no labels. An intercept-only calibrator, which estimates no slope, also benefits from a second donor (+21.14 on the gradient-boosted predictor (631.03 to 609.89), +31.86 on ridge (597.21 to 565.35)), which rules out an explanation based on slope conditioning alone.

The ranking of calibrators is not stable across base predictors: affine passes correction on the gradient-boosted predictor while isotonic does not, and the reverse holds on ridge. No claim about which calibrator is best is made.

### 4.5 Practical utility

A leave-one-condition-out procedure that selects the calibrator and donor count on training conditions and evaluates on the held-out condition chose two donors in 11 of 11 folds on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor, 10 of 11 with ridge, and in 57 of 57 folds on ISU. Its error matched the best fixed strategy exactly on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor (454.33) and on ISU (2.470), and was 7.40 worse on UConn with ridge (499.98 against 492.58, p = 0.34).

Comparing best achievable with one donor against best achievable with two: 462.04 against 454.33 on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor, 514.00 against 492.58 with ridge, and 2.542 against 2.470 on ISU. The donor decision is therefore learnable from training conditions alone and worth about 2 to 4% of error, but it does not beat the best fixed strategy, because it converges to it.

## 5 Five implementation defects

Five errors in our own analysis were found by an independent model reading our code. None was found by self-inspection.

A label-budget experiment drew a single random sample per configuration and reported it as an expectation; apparent non-monotonicity in the p-values disappeared once a thousand draws were used. A donor-axis experiment contained no Monte-Carlo loop at all, so its mean-versus-median contrast aggregated across evaluated cells rather than across draws and was not the pre-registered sensitivity test. A matched-budget experiment allocated rows as floor(k/m) per donor, so at k = 8 with m = 3 the total was six rows rather than eight, and the resulting finding that spreading labels too thin hurts reversed once exact allocation was used. A cross-dataset replication drew one-donor and two-donor arms from different donor pools, which produced an apparent dataset disagreement that disappeared under a common support. A ridge control standardised features on the full dataset before splitting, leaking test-fold statistics into training.

All five share a property: they are invisible in a results table and visible in the code. We recommend that post-processing pipelines of this kind be reviewed by a reader who did not write them, and we note that in our case the reviewer was a different model with access to the same files.

## 6 Limitations

Two datasets, one predictor family, one task definition. The condition is the statistical unit, giving n = 11 on UConn and 57 on ISU for the main contrasts, and a minimum detectable effect near 0.30 MAE on UConn. The single-donor row test has three paired units and cannot decide saturation either way. The donor effect is small in relative terms (3 to 6% of baseline), the calibrator ranking is unstable across base predictors, and the residual-borrowing probe is diagnostic rather than a method. ISU RUL is in reference-test counts, not cycles, because no cycle axis is distributed with that dataset. Nothing here speaks to how the conclusions change with a different predictor family or with physical, calendar-time end-of-life definitions.

## 7 Conclusion

At a fixed budget of eight labels drawn from other cells of the target operating condition, a second donor cell buys a small and estimator-dependent improvement in calibration error. The direction reverses for an estimator that borrows a single raw label, survives stratification by remaining life, and reverses again on a query subset defined by donor availability. The donor-count decision can be learned from training conditions and is worth a few percent, but it does not displace the best fixed strategy. We report these boundaries rather than a recommendation, together with five implementation defects that independent review, not self-inspection, brought to light.
