# Donor Allocation at a Fixed Label Budget: Estimator Dependence and Population Boundaries in Battery RUL Calibration

> Draft v4.0 — 2026-11-08. Every number traceable to `.research/exp/EXP-001..021`. Scope fixed by CD-015: three claims only.

## Abstract
Cross-condition battery remaining-useful-life models are usually evaluated under leave-one-condition-out, which gives the model no labels from the target condition. In deployment, labels always arrive first. We study their allocation at a fixed budget of eight labelled rows, from one donor cell or spread across two.

Across 64 LFP cells in 11 operating conditions and 248 cells in 63 conditions, with remaining life in cumulative cycles, moving from one to two donor cells reduced mean absolute calibration error by 16.67 cycles (11/11 conditions, Holm p = 0.0002) and 0.156 units (44/57, Holm 0.0003) under affine calibration, and by 6.63 (8/11, not significant) and 0.085 (39/57, Holm 0.0005) under isotonic calibration. The effect is estimator-dependent: a nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest point moved the opposite way on both datasets (-7.82 and -0.063); borrowing the residual attenuated that harm to +0.53 and +0.001.

The effect is not a unit artifact, is not confined to linear calibration, and keeps its sign across all four remaining-life quartiles. It has population boundaries: on a query subset defined by donor availability, affine and isotonic effects reverse under both restricted and unrestricted donor pools, and no pool-by-count interaction survives correction. Leave-one-condition-out selection recovered two donors in every fold, without beating the best fixed strategy. No universal donor-count or calibrator recommendation follows. A sensitivity budget over five post-processing decisions shows that two, the scope of the fit and the population evaluated, move the contrast by 274% and by a sign reversal, while three others move it by at most 12%.
## 1 Introduction

Battery life models are validated by holding out an operating condition and reporting error on it. The protocol is clean and it answers a real question: how well does this model transfer to a condition it has never seen? But it is not the question a deployment engineer faces. A newly deployed condition produces data immediately, and the practical question is how much of it must be labelled before the model becomes usable.

That question has two axes. The number of labelled rows k can be increased at almost no cost, because a completed cell trajectory can be mined retrospectively for many label points. The number of distinct donor cells m is expensive: each additional donor is another cell that must be run to end of life before its labels exist. If the two axes were interchangeable, one would always buy the cheap one. Our starting hypothesis was that they are not, and that there is a floor on the row axis.

The floor did not appear. Rows kept helping beyond the point where our design had power to detect saturation. What did appear, and what this paper is about, is that the donor axis has an effect whose sign depends on the estimator and whose magnitude depends on the population you evaluate it on.

The paper makes three claims, and only three.

First, at a fixed budget of eight labelled rows from other cells of the target condition, increasing the donor count from one to two produced modest average improvements for affine and isotonic calibration on two datasets, with unequal statistical support.

Second, the donor-count effect depends on the estimator. A nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest calibration point showed the opposite direction; borrowing the residual instead attenuated that harm. These controls constrain the possible mechanisms without establishing that aggregation alone causes the difference.

Third, the full-population benefit has population boundaries. Stratifying by RUL quartile preserves the sign of every effect, but a subset of queries selected by a donor-availability constraint reverses the affine and isotonic effects under both donor pools.

## 2 Related work

Supervision budgets for cross-condition battery models have been probed before, but usually as a robustness check rather than as the object of study. Zhao (2026) scans five, ten and twenty target cells for scalar cycle-life transfer and concludes that large budgets are unnecessary; its unit is a cell, and its target is a single number per cell. Su et al. (2025) frame the budget as a percentage of lifetime data and report that a small fraction suffices for state-of-health transfer. BatLiNet (2025) varies the number of source-side reference cells from one to sixty-four to reduce variance in its median prediction, and settles on sixty-four for inference speed; its references carry no target labels.

Our setting differs in three ways that matter for interpretation. The budget is counted in labelled rows from *other cells of the target condition*, so calibration and evaluation cells are disjoint by construction. The target is a trajectory rather than a scalar, so one donor contributes many label points. And the comparison is made at an exactly matched row budget, so a donor-count difference cannot be attributed to row count.

The estimator dependence we observe is a special case of a known phenomenon: local methods are sensitive to the density and homogeneity of the calibration set, while parametric fits average over it. Work on local gradient correction for tabular regression makes the same distinction. We do not claim a new estimator; we use the contrast as a probe.

## 3 Protocol

### 3.1 Data

UConn-ISU-ILCC: 64 LFP cells in 11 operating-condition groups, characterised at up to 77 reference performance tests, each providing a 100 s pulse at 20/50/90% state of charge in both directions and a constant-current discharge curve. RUL is expressed in cumulative cycles: the per-interval cycle count is a segment length, and its cumulative sum gives a monotone aging clock (Spearman correlation between state of health and cumulative cycles is -1.0000 in 52 of 64 cells). Usable queries: 2,813, with RUL from 0 to 8,425 cycles (median 2,234).

ISU-ILCC (second dataset): 248 of 251 cells parse, in 63 condition groups, 5,497 reference-test rows of which 3,422 are usable for RUL; every cell reaches 80% state of health. Features are four capacity channels. RUL is in reference-test counts (0-42) because a cycle-count axis is not distributed with this dataset.

### 3.2 Predictor and calibration

One gradient-boosted regressor per dataset, held fixed throughout; all calibration experiments are post-processing of its leave-one-condition-out predictions. A second frozen predictor (ridge regression, features standardised inside each training fold) is used as a control.

Calibration maps the predictor output to the target using k labelled rows drawn only from cells other than the one being evaluated. Four calibrators: an intercept-only shift, an affine map, isotonic regression, and a nearest-neighbour rule that borrows either the raw label or the residual of the nearest calibration point in prediction space.

### 3.3 Statistics

The statistical unit is the operating condition, never the cell or the query. Unless stated otherwise, queries are averaged within each cell and then across cells, so every arm uses identical weights; where an earlier run used query-weighted averaging instead, the text says so and reports both. Monte-Carlo draws are aggregated with the mean (primary) and the median (mandatory sensitivity). Multiplicity is controlled by Holm correction with the cumulative maximum, over pre-declared families.

### 3.4 Protocol lock

Four degrees of freedom were frozen before the main experiments: the aggregation rule over Monte-Carlo draws, the scope of the affine fit (per evaluated cell or shared per condition), donor eligibility, and the sampling scheme. One choice was not pre-registered: mean aggregation over draws was adopted as the primary summary after inspecting results, with the median retained as a mandatory sensitivity, and the main table reports the mean with median values given in Section 4.5. We state this as a post-hoc decision rather than a pre-registered one. Maximum cross-configuration drift was 0.018 MAE against a within-configuration seed standard deviation of 0.0063.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Donor count, two datasets

At an exactly matched budget of eight labelled rows, with a common donor pool and the same evaluable cells in both arms. Mean and median aggregation agree in sign and in which calibrators reach correction.

The same effects computed in reference-test counts on UConn (affine +0.21, isotonic +0.07, 1-NN -0.06) are within 1.44% versus 1.43% of baseline for isotonic, so the conclusion does not depend on the unit.

The single-donor row axis did not reach the pre-registered equivalence margin. Fixing one donor and adding rows from k = 16 to k = 64 changed error by +0.108 and +0.146 (mean, two seeds) and +0.033 and +0.074 (median); the mean point estimates and both median point estimates exceed 0.05, and every interval's upper bound does. A separate single-donor probe in an earlier configuration gave -0.062 (p = 0.0033, 17 paired units) and was read at the time as evidence of saturation at k of about 16; we do not sustain that reading, because it rests on the same thin donor-availability base and its sign is opposite to the current estimate. The test is weak for a structural reason: only 9 of 64 cells have at least 64 rows and they fall in three conditions, so the primary test has three paired units. We therefore report it as inconclusive rather than as evidence that rows do not matter.

### 4.2 Estimator dependence

At the same budget and on the same queries, a nearest-neighbour calibrator that borrows the raw label of the nearest calibration point moves in the opposite direction on both datasets. Replacing raw-label borrowing with residual borrowing, in which the nearest point contributes y - p and that residual is added to the test prediction, attenuates the harm to +0.53 (UConn) and +0.001 (ISU).

This is consistent with a level-versus-shape distinction: estimators that aggregate over calibration points average out donor-specific offsets, while a nearest-neighbour rule selects one point and therefore inherits the offset without averaging. The comparison is a probe, not a method: residual borrowing is the worst of the four calibrators on ISU (2.939) but not on UConn, where the raw-label rule is worse (535.51 against 528.26).

We note an algebraic caveat. Residual borrowing removes the neighbour's prediction displacement but retains its residual bias, and at the neighbour's own prediction the two rules coincide. The contrast therefore constrains possible mechanisms; it does not identify aggregation as the cause.

### 4.3 Population boundaries

Stratifying queries by RUL quartile (cut points 1,144, 2,234, 3,430 cycles) preserves the sign of every effect: intercept +28.81/+21.08/+17.12/+17.55, affine +11.53/+27.00/+27.88/+22.39, isotonic +7.29/+12.83/+9.39/+7.39, 1-NN -10.41/-7.41/-5.88/-11.48. Sixteen cells, no sign exception. Individual cells are underpowered at n = 11 conditions.

A donor-availability constraint, in which only donors whose trajectory is complete by the target's current cycle may be used, restricts evaluation to 199 queries. Under cell-macro weighting the affine and isotonic effects on that subset are -21.57 (2/11 conditions improved) and -24.92 (0/11); an earlier run of the same comparison that weighted queries rather than cells gave -21.76 and -16.85, and the isotonic figure differs materially between the two weightings, which we report rather than reconcile. A factorial comparison on those same 199 queries, crossing donor pool (restricted or unrestricted) with donor count and using cell-macro weighting throughout, finds no interaction: all four pool-by-count interactions are null after Holm correction, and the unrestricted pool also reverses on that subset (-28.03 affine, -23.03 isotonic). The reversal therefore belongs to the query subset, not to the constraint.

### 4.4 Controls

An uncalibrated arm is invariant to donor count (difference exactly zero, 0/11), as it must be, since it uses no labels. An intercept-only calibrator, which estimates no slope, also benefits from a second donor (+21.14 on the gradient-boosted predictor (631.03 to 609.89), +31.86 on ridge (597.21 to 565.35)), which rules out an explanation based on slope conditioning alone.

The ranking of calibrators is not stable across base predictors: affine passes correction on the gradient-boosted predictor while isotonic does not, and the reverse holds on ridge. No claim about which calibrator is best is made.

### 4.5 Practical utility

A leave-one-condition-out procedure that selects the calibrator and donor count on training conditions and evaluates on the held-out condition chose two donors in every fold on every dataset: 11 of 11 on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor, 11 of 11 with ridge (ten selecting affine with two donors and one selecting isotonic with two), and 57 of 57 on ISU. Its error matched the best fixed strategy exactly on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor (454.33) and on ISU (2.470), and was 7.40 worse on UConn with ridge (499.98 against 492.58, p = 0.34).

Comparing best achievable with one donor against best achievable with two: 462.04 against 454.33 on UConn with the gradient-boosted predictor, 514.00 against 492.58 with ridge, and 2.542 against 2.470 on ISU. The UConn isotonic two-donor figure is 454.33 in this selection experiment and 453.33 in the arm table of Section 4.1; the two come from separate runs with different draw counts and are not interchangeable. The donor decision is therefore learnable from training conditions alone and worth about 2 to 4% of error, but it does not beat the best fixed strategy, because it converges to it.

## 5 A sensitivity budget for calibration post-processing

The calibration layer is post-processing: it takes a fixed predictor's output and maps it to the target using a handful of labelled rows. Its reported effect therefore depends on choices that are not part of the method and are rarely stated. We measured how much each choice moves the answer.

We fix one headline contrast - UConn, remaining life in cycles, eight labels, affine calibration, one donor against two - and vary one decision at a time.

| Decision varied | Contrast | Change from baseline |
|---|---:|---:|
| baseline: cell-macro, mean over draws, per-cell affine, all queries, exact row allocation | +18.34 | - |
| aggregation order changed to query pooling | +20.57 | +2.23 (+12%) |
| draw aggregation changed to median | +17.26 | -1.07 (-6%) |
| affine scope changed to one fit per condition | +68.65 | +50.32 (+274%) |
| evaluation restricted to the late-life quartile | -19.86 | -38.19 (-208%) |
| row allocation changed to floor(k/m) per donor | +16.55 | -1.79 (-10%) |
| | | |
| **ISU, same scan** | **contrast** | **change** |
| baseline | +0.089 | - |
| aggregation order changed to query pooling | +0.066 | -0.023 (-26%) |
| draw aggregation changed to median | +0.051 | -0.038 (-43%) |
| affine scope changed to one fit per condition | +0.213 | +0.124 (+140%) |
| evaluation restricted to the late-life quartile | -0.207 | -0.296 (-332%) |
| row allocation changed to floor(k/m) per donor | +0.114 | +0.025 (+28%) |

Repeating the scan on ISU, with its own predictor and its own unit of remaining life, gives the same ranking. Changing the scope of the affine fit inflates the contrast by 140% there (274% on UConn); restricting evaluation to the late-life quartile reverses its sign there too (-332% against -208% on UConn, p = 0.0007). The three remaining decisions move the contrast by 26 to 43% on ISU and 6 to 12% on UConn, and their sign is not stable across the two datasets.

The practical reading is that a disagreement between two studies about whether an extra donor helps need not reflect a difference in data. It can reflect which of these five choices each study made silently. Two of the five - the scope of the fit and the population evaluated - are worth stating in any report of a calibration result; the other three can be standardised without loss.

The scope effect deserves a caveat: sharing one affine fit across all cells of a condition changes the absolute error level as well as the contrast, so the 274% figure mixes a level shift with a change in the contrast itself. The population effect has no such ambiguity: the sign reverses, and the late-life contrast is not significant (p = 0.09) in either direction, so what the table records is that the reported direction is population-dependent.
## 6 Limitations

ISU supplies 63 conditions in total; 57 enter the donor-count contrasts because six have fewer than two eligible donor cells under the eight-row budget, and the exclusion is applied identically to every arm. Two datasets, one predictor family, one task definition. The condition is the statistical unit, giving n = 11 on UConn and 57 on ISU for the main contrasts, and a minimum detectable effect near 0.30 MAE on UConn. The single-donor row test has three paired units and cannot decide saturation either way. The donor effect is small in relative terms (3 to 6% of baseline), the calibrator ranking is unstable across base predictors, and the residual-borrowing probe is diagnostic rather than a method. ISU RUL is in reference-test counts, not cycles, because no cycle axis is distributed with that dataset. Nothing here speaks to how the conclusions change with a different predictor family or with physical, calendar-time end-of-life definitions.

## 7 Conclusion

At a fixed budget of eight labels drawn from other cells of the target operating condition, a second donor cell buys a small and estimator-dependent improvement in calibration error. The direction reverses for an estimator that borrows a single raw label, survives stratification by remaining life, and reverses again on a query subset defined by donor availability. The donor-count decision can be learned from training conditions and is worth a few percent, but it does not displace the best fixed strategy. We report these boundaries rather than a recommendation.
