Methods for Lithium-Ion Traction Batteries in Vehicular 
Applications: Task Definition, Benchmark Evaluation, 
and Deployment Challenges 
 
Haozhe Gao* 
School of Technology, Beijing Forestry University, Beijing 100083, China

Abstract 
The state of health (SOH) of traction batteries is a common basis for safety warning, residual value assessment, 
and second-life utilization of electric vehicles, but a gap exists between the laboratory accuracy of existing 
deep learning methods and their  on-board usability. This paper reviews the relevant research progress using 
on-board usability as the screening criterion. SOH estimation, SOH prediction, and remaining useful life (RUL) 
prediction are distinguished according to label source and evaluatio n protocol, and the limits on the 
comparability of error values are explained. Existing methods are grouped into six categories by modeling 
mechanism and evaluated category by category using four on-board questions: input availability, cross-domain 
generalization validation, uncertainty output, and edge -computing resource constraints. Six models are 
compared on the CALCE CS2 dataset under random splitting, temporal extrapolation, and cell -level holdout 
protocols. Under random splitting, the mean absolute errors of all six models do not exceed 0.0150. Under 
temporal extrapolation, they range from 0.0204 to 0.1392, and the R²  values of three neural networks become 
negative. Under cell-level holdout, they range from 0.0254 to 0.0378. Under the CALCE CS2 dataset and the 
settings of this paper, protocol selection changes both the error level and the model ranking. 
Keywords 
lithium-ion traction battery, state -of-health estimation, state -of-health prediction, deep learning, on -board 
usability, data splitting protocol 
 
 
1. Introduction 
1.1 Engineering Requirements for On-Board Traction Battery State-of-Health Estimation 
New energy vehicles and traction batteries are key components of transport decarbonization [1]. Battery 
state of health (SOH) determines driving range, charging and discharging capability, and safety limits [2], 
while practical vehicle requirements focus on degradation warning, residual value pricing, and admission to 
second-life utilization [3]. On -board conditions differ from laboratory conditions. A battery management 
system (BMS) collects only voltage, current, and temperature, and a complete constant-voltage charging stage 
is difficult to obtain [4]. Real-world operating conditions vary with driving behavior and ambient temperature,

1.2 Coverage and Limitations of Existing Reviews 
Zhao et al. [3], Ma et al. [6], and Hu et al. [7] summarized battery and SOH estimation methods. Tang et 
al. [8], Yao et al. [9], and Luo et al. [10] reviewed estimation and prediction methods. Noura et al. [11] focused 
on hybrid electric vehicle scenarios, Wang et al. [12] discussed online estimation, and Xie et al. [13] covered 
whole-life prediction. 
The limitations of existing reviews are concentrated at the validation level. Task boundaries are unclear, 
and state of charge and state of health are often discussed in parallel [14]. The comparability of estimation and 
prediction errors is not adequately explained. Classification is based on network names, making hybrid 
architectures difficult to categorize [15]. Performance indicators are mostly extracted from different sources, 
so the validity of horizontal comparisons is limited [16]. 
1.3 Work and Contributions of This Paper 
This paper addresses four questions. RQ1 concerns the definitions and evaluation protocols of the three 
SOH tasks. RQ2 asks whether each category of deep learning methods remains valid under on -board 
constraints. RQ3 asks whether the splitting protocol cha nges the relative conclusions among methods. RQ4 
asks what evidence is still lacking for on-board deployment. 
The work of this paper includes distinguishing three types of SOH tasks, evaluating methods using four on-
board questions (input availability, cross -domain generalization validation, uncertainty output, and edge -
computing resource constraints), and comparing six models on the CALCE CS2 dataset under three protocols. 
2. Problem Definition, Data, and Evaluation Protocols 
2.1 Definition of SOH and Health Indicators 
SOH has two definitions: capacity retention, which is the ratio of current available capacity to initial 
capacity, and internal resistance growth, which reflects degradation of power capability [2-3, 17]. Health 
indicators are commonly extracted from charging and discharging curves, including the peak height and peak 
position of the incremental capacity (IC) curve, differential voltage curve features, the voltage rise rate during 
constant-current charging, and statistics from partial charging intervals [17-20]. Electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy requires dedicated excitation equipment and is difficult to obtain routinely under on -board 
conditions [21]. Indicators that depend on complete charging and discharging curves are often missing in real 
vehicles. Indicators from partial charging segments are closer to on -board conditions but are affected by the 
initial state of charge and temperature [19, 22]. 
2.2 Distinction Among the Three Tasks 
The three tasks differ in label source and evaluation requirements, as shown in Table 1 (corresponding to 
RQ1). SOH estimation infers the current state from current measurements, and its label is obtained from 
capacity calibration in the same cycle. SOH prediction extrapolates the SOH trajectory over future cycles [13, 
23]. RUL prediction outputs the number of remaining cycles until the end-of-life point [24-25]. Only the latter 
two must use temporal extrapolation. Because their label sources differ, the errors of estimation and prediction 
cannot be compared directly [16].

2.3 Public Datasets and On-Board Representativeness 
NASA PCoE and CALCE are the most widely used datasets. The former uses constant -current charging 
and discharging at room temperature, whereas the latter includes multiple rates and temperatures. The 
experiments in this paper use the CS2 subset (room temper ature, approximately 0.5C). The Oxford dataset 
uses urban-driving current profiles, and MIT -Stanford covers a relatively broad life distribution [26]. Real-
vehicle and retired -battery datasets are larger, such as multimodal datasets containing driving and charging 
records [27]. 
The limitations of existing datasets include the following: most contain individual cells; coverage of 
combinations of temperature and operating conditions is limited; and the charging process is controlled and 
complete, unlike interrupted charging in real  vehicles [27-28]. The operating -condition coverage of the

2.4 Evaluation Metrics and Data Splitting Protocols 
Error magnitude is measured by mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean 
absolute percentage error (MAPE). The first two use the SOH ratio as the unit, MAPE is expressed as a 
percentage, and goodness of fit is measured by the coefficient of determination (R² ) [13, 29]. The experiments 
in this paper report MAE, MAPE, and R² . 
Data splitting protocols are divided into three types. Random splitting shuffles all samples before splitting, 
so adjacent cycles of the same cell may fall on opposite sides of the split. Cell-level holdout uses an entire cell 
as the unit. Temporal extrapolation uses early cycles for training and later cycles for testing [11, 16]. Random 
splitting corresponds to interpolation within the same cell and can only serve as a baseline; it does not constitute 
evidence of generalization performance. Errors under the three protocols are not directly comparable.

3.2 Input Representation and Health Factor Construction 
There are three types of input representation: voltage, current, and temperature time series [33-34]; health 
factors, including IC-curve peak features and equal-voltage-rise charging time [18, 35]; and local curves from 
charging segments [19-20, 22]. Availability increases in this order. The third type is closest to real -vehicle 
conditions but is most sensitive to the initial state of charge and temperature and therefore requires explicit 
normalization [22]. Mode decomposition can suppress noise [36], but the number of modes depends on 
experience [37]. 
3.3 Convolutional and Temporal Models 
Convolutional networks extract curve shapes through local receptive fields [38]. Temporal convolutional 
networks enlarge the receptive field through dilated convolution and have been used under multiple -
temperature conditions [34]. LSTM networks and gated recurrent units extract temporal dependencies [33, 39],

while the combination of attention and recurrent structures increases the weights of key time steps [40]. The 
generalization boundary of these methods is unclear: the model learns the degradation trajectories of the 
training cells rather than transferable aging laws. 
3.4 Attention and Transformer Models 
Transformers model long-range dependencies through self-attention [41-42]. Sparse attention and sequence 
decomposition reduce complexity [43]. Graph convolutional networks can estimate SOH and RUL 
simultaneously [44]. Their advantage lies in representing long -sequence inputs, at the cost of high parameter 
counts and memory usage. 
3.5 Hybrid Architectures 
Hybrid architectures connect local feature extraction with long -range dependency modeling [18, 40, 45-
47]. Several studies have reported low errors for such methods under within-cell interpolation settings, but the 
structures are complex, contain many hyperparameters, and depend on broad training-data coverage for cross-
cell generalization [15]. Dual-branch structures also increase parameter count and latency. 
3.6 Physics-Informed Fusion 
Physics-informed fusion introduces mechanistic knowledge through constraints, loss terms, or network 
structures [48-52]. Recent studies have combined it with Kolmogorov -Arnold networks [31-32, 53]. 
Mechanistic constraints limit predictions to physically feasible ranges, and performance outside the training 
interval is generally better than that of purely data-driven methods. Its limitations are the high cost of parameter 
identification and the limited transferability of mechanistic terms because battery aging mechanisms vary with 
chemistry and operating conditions [5]. 
3.7 Transfer Learning, Meta-Learning, and Domain Generalization 
Transfer learning transfers knowledge from a source domain to a target cell through feature alignment or 
parameter fine-tuning, and the main difficulty is the difference in data distributions. Domain adaptation and 
domain generalization relax the condition s for using target -domain data [54], while meta-learning addresses 
small-sample scenarios [55]. These methods respond to cross -domain shortcomings, but their effectiveness 
depends on the similarity between source and target domains. If both are adjacent cycles from the same group 
of cells, protocol leakage exaggerates the benefit [16]. 
3.8 Generative and Foundation Models 
A generative pre-trained Transformer can predict degradation trajectories from early cycles [23]. A large 
language model combined with a multi-time-scale Transformer [56], a Transformer combined with a diffusion 
model [57], and a prompt -driven large language model [58] have been applied to SOH estimation and 
prediction. Their advantage lies in knowledge reuse across chemistries and in label -sparse scenarios. Their 
limitations are inference costs far beyond the BMS budget and a lack of constraints on physical consistency  
[23, 58].

4.2 Dataset and Feature Extraction 
The experiments use the CALCE CS2 dataset, which contains six 18650 cells, CS2_33 through CS2_38, 
with a rated capacity of 1.1 Ah and cycling at approximately 0.5C at room temperature. The discharge capacity 
of a single cycle is calculated from the increme nts within the cycle, and the SOH label is the ratio of the 
discharge capacity of that cycle to the discharge capacity of the first cycle. 
The input features are taken only from the charging stage. The 12 scalar features include charging duration, 
initial voltage, final voltage, maximum voltage, voltage mean and standard deviation, the times required to 
reach 3.8 V, 4.0 V, 4.1 V, and 4.2 V, a nd the time differences between intervals. A 64 -dimensional sequence 
obtained by resampling the charging curve is also used. After cycles with abnormal capacities and samples 
with labels outside the range of 0.50 to 1.20 were removed, 4,908 samples remained. Each cell contained 711 
to 932 valid cycles, and SOH ranged from 0.50 to 1.12. These features are extracted from controlled, complete 
charging stages and require a relatively complete charging process. Therefore, the controlled experiments in 
this Section are used only to test the effect of data splitting protocols on the conclusions, not to demonstrate 
on-board input availability or deployment feasibility. Partial-charging inputs are discussed in Section 5. 
To examine the effect of reduced input information, an input variant with a 4.0 V truncated window was 
constructed using the same procedure. Only sampling points with charging current greater than 0.05 A and 
voltage below 4.0 V were retained. The voltage t hresholds of the 12 -dimensional features were shifted 
accordingly to 3.6 V, 3.8 V, 3.9 V, and 4.0 V, and the curve was resampled within the same window. Both 
input types used the same sample -screening rules. The truncated window retained 4,692 samples, 216  fewer 
than the complete-charging features; the difference came from cycles with fewer than 30 valid sampling points 
below 4.0 V. 
4.3 Three Splitting Protocols and Comparison Models 
Random splitting shuffles all samples and divides them at an 8:2 ratio. Test samples lie among training 
samples, so the model only needs to interpolate. Temporal extrapolation uses the first 70% of cycles of each 
cell for training and the last 30% for test ing. Cell-level holdout uses an entire cell as the test unit, with five 
cells used for training and one for testing in each fold, rotating through all six cells. The splits under all three 
protocols are fixed. Random splitting uses seed 42 to generate one 8:2 split; temporal extrapolation is 
determined by cycle order; and cell -level holdout is determined by cell identifier. Three repetitions refer to 
model-side random seeds 0, 1, and 2, which affect feature subsampling in the random forest, weight 
initialization in the shallow neural network and the two deep models, and subsampling of the Gaussian process 
regression training set. 
The comparison includes four conventional models and two deep models. Support vector regression uses a 
radial basis function kernel, penalty coefficient C = 10, kernel coefficient γ set to the scikit-learn ‘scale’ value, 
and epsilon-insensitive loss ε = 0. 005. Gaussian process regression uses a fixed combination of a constant 
kernel, a radial basis function kernel (length scale 5.0), and a white -noise kernel (noise level 10 -3), without 
kernel-parameter optimization. Because the computational complexity of exact inference is O(n 3), its training 
subset is limited to 900 samples. The random forest contains 200 trees. The shallow neural network has two 
hidden layers with 64 and 32 neurons. It is optimized with Adam using a learning rate of 10 -3 for a maximum 
of 1,500 epochs; 10% of the training set is used for internal validation, with early stopping after 40 consecutive 
epochs without improvement. The one -dimensional convolutional neural network (1D -CNN) contains two 
convolutional layers follow ed by adaptive pooling. The LSTM network has a 32 -dimensional hidden state. 
Both are trained for 120 epochs with Adam, a learning rate of 3 ×  10-3, a batch size of 128, and mean squared 
error loss. In the deep models, the output of the convolutional or recurrent layer is concatenated with the 12 -
dimensional scalar features and passed to a fully connected output layer. Inputs are standardized using statistics 
from the training set. The evaluation metrics are MAE, MAPE, and R² . All hyperparameters take values 
commonly used in the literature and are determined only from the training set and its internal validation subset; 
the test set is not used for tuning. No independent validation set is used except for the internal validation of the 
shallow neural network.

4.5 Discussion and Limitations 
The difference between random splitting and temporal extrapolation is the difference between interpolation 
and extrapolation. The difference between temporal extrapolation and cell -level holdout lies in the 
extrapolation target, and the former shows a more  pronounced increase in error. Under random splitting, test 
samples fall inside the training interval; the fact that training and test samples come from the same source is 
only one manifestation of this problem. 
Therefore, under the CALCE CS2 dataset and the settings of this paper, random splitting reduces the 
reported error level and changes the model ranking. Its values should not be used as evidence of generalization 
performance. 
The experimental limitations are as follows: the data come from a single dataset and a single rate; model 
scale and training epochs are simplified to control cost, and no independent validation set is used except for 
the internal validation of the shallow neural network; temporal extrapolation tests only the same rate; the 
truncated window still comes from controlled charging and differs from randomly interrupted segments in real 
vehicles; and repeated splits and repeated holdouts are not performed, so cros s-cell variation reflects only the 
six available cells. 
5. Challenges and Research Directions for On-Board Deployment 
This Section summarizes common limitations according to the four on -board questions (Table 6, 
corresponding to RQ4). Literature evidence and experimental evidence are taken from the literature matrix in 
Section 2 and the experiments in Section 4, respectively. 
5.1 Availability of Input Signals 
Among the 120 papers included in the statistics, 93 use individual cells as the application scenario, and 
only nine involve partial charging segments (as judged by title). For input availability, 18 titles matched, of 
which 12 were confirmed and six were e xcluded after individual verification. Methods that rely on 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy require dedicated excitation equipment [21], and methods that rely 
on a complete constant-voltage charging stage fail when charging is interrupted [20]. Partial charging segments 
are the easiest inputs to obtain. A feasible direction is to construct health factors around them and use the initial 
state of charge and ambient temperature as explicit inputs [19, 22]. The difficulty lies in maintaining stability 
when segment length and initial conditions vary simultaneously.

5.2 Generalization Across Cells and Operating Conditions 
Based on title screening, only nine papers involve cross-domain transfer or generalization, seven of which 
belong to the transfer and domain-generalization family. Individual verification confirmed that all nine provide 
validation across cells, datasets, or operating conditions. The experiments in Section 4 show that errors increase 
by factors of 1.8-12.5 after switching to temporal extrapolation. Transfer learning, domain generalization, and 
meta-learning provide feasible paths [54, 55], but their effectiveness depends on the similarity between source 
and target domains. When the protocol does not strictly separate the two, leakage exaggerates the benefit [16]. 
A prerequisite is a public benchmark covering multiple chemistries, temperatures, and rates [27]. 
5.3 Insufficient Data in the Early-Life Stage 
Labels are scarce during the early stage of battery use, whereas users and vehicle manufacturers most need 
early judgments. Generative and foundation models reduce label dependence through pre -training [23, 58], 
meta-learning has advantages with few samples [55], and the MIT-Stanford dataset covers a relatively broad 
life distribution [26]. However, early-life windows range from tens to hundreds of cycles across studies, and 
no conclusion has been reached regarding the trade-off between window length and accuracy. 
5.4 Uncertainty and Interpretability 
BMS safety warning and residual value assessment require interval estimates. Existing studies provide 
prediction intervals through randomized methods and Bayesian neural networks [59-60], but the stability of 
interval coverage under cross -domain conditions has not been validated. Of five title hits, only two were 
confirmed to output intervals or probabilities. Although the titles of the other three contained ‘interval’ or 
‘stochastic,’ these terms actually referred to a time scale or a stochastic configuration network. Attention 
weights and the physical meanings of health factors lack strict correspondence, making the basis of model 
judgments difficult to trace. Review studies have also identified interpretability as an unresolved issue [61]. 
5.5 Edge Computing and Real-Time Performance 
The computing and storage budgets of BMS main controllers are limited. Lightweight methods have been 
developed through pruning, distillation, and fixed -point quantization [4], but individual verification of six 
papers whose titles involved edge deployment or real -time performance found that none reported measured 
inference latency, computing -resource usage, or an embedded implementation. Among the 120 papers 
included in this paper, title-level screening also found no measured comparison of computing performance on 
a specific BMS platform. 
5.6 Research Opportunities and Decision Criteria 
The following directions should be prioritized. First, public evaluation benchmarks with protocol labels 
should be established to cover multiple chemistries, temperatures, and rates, and errors under all three protocols 
should be reported. Second, conditio n-adaptive models for partial charging segments should be developed, 
with initial state of charge and ambient temperature used as explicit inputs, while ensuring that error variation 
remains acceptable when segment length and initial conditions change. Thi rd, uncertainty should be 
incorporated into standard evaluation metrics, with both interval coverage and width reported [16, 60].

6. Conclusions 
At the task level, this paper distinguishes SOH estimation, SOH prediction, and RUL prediction and 
explains why high accuracy in an estimation task does not support prediction capability. At the method level, 
existing approaches are grouped into six categories according to modeling mechanism. A two-step procedure 
of title screening followed by verification of abstracts and experimental settings was conducted according to 
the four on -board questions (Figure 3), and all 36 matching papers were checked. All ni ne papers on cross -
domain generalization were confirmed. Twelve of 18 papers on input availability were confirmed, whereas the 
confirmed counts for uncertainty output and edge-computing constraints were two and zero, respectively, and 
the confirmed counts were still lower within the six method families. Physics-informed fusion has advantages 
in cross-condition extrapolation, and generative and foundation models have potential in label-scarce scenarios, 
but their inference costs exceed the BMS budget. 
The comparison under three protocols provides the most direct evidence. The MAEs of all six models do 
not exceed 0.0150 under random splitting. Under temporal extrapolation, errors increase by factors of 1.8-12.5 
and three neural networks produce negative R² values. Under cell-level holdout, errors increase by factors of 
1.9-4.7. The comparison using the 4.0 V truncated window shows that shortening the input mainly reduces 
within-cell interpolation accuracy (errors increase by factors of 1.3 -2.3) and does not  change the relative 
relationship among protocols. The protocol changes both the error level and the model ranking. Under the 
CALCE CS2 dataset and the settings of this paper, the influence of protocol selection on the conclusion is no 
less than that of model selection. 
Accordingly, on -board SOH methods should use temporal extrapolation as the minimum validation 
requirement and report the splitting unit and metric definitions. Uncertainty and edge -resource usage should 
be standard reporting items. The evidence still lacki ng for on -board deployment is concentrated on input 
stability under real charging behavior, interval coverage under cross-domain conditions, and data on the trade-
off between accuracy and computing resources for specific BMS platforms.
